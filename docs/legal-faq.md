## Foundations: terminology to keep straight

### Q: What's the difference between sound recording copyright and composition copyright?

Two separate copyrights exist for every recorded song:

- **Composition copyright** — owned by the songwriter / publisher. Covers the underlying musical work (melody, lyrics, chord progression as written).
- **Sound recording copyright** (a.k.a. "master") — owned by the producer / record label. Covers a *particular fixed recording* of the composition.

A user who uploads a recording to StemScriber is invoking both: the composition (the song itself) and the sound recording (this specific recorded version). Any analysis of "do we need permission" must consider both layers.

**Sources:** NYC Bar Primer pp. 1-2 ("two separate and distinct intellectual properties... a user will have to consider obtaining separate rights for both the use of the song and the sound recording"); Passman ch 20 (Sound Recording Copyrights); 17 U.S.C. § 102, § 106.

### Q: Does the music-industry term "transcription license" apply to what StemScriber does?

**No.** This is a vocabulary trap. In music-industry usage, a "transcription license" means an *audio-only sync license* — licensing a song for use in radio, podcasts, or audio commercials. It has nothing to do with chord-charting from audio.

**What StemScriber does (audio → chord chart / MIDI / sheet music)** is governed by an entirely different framework:
- Sound recording reproduction rights (when audio is uploaded/processed)
- Derivative work rights (deriving notation from a recording)
- Fair use / transformative use doctrine

Implication: when reading music-industry guides, "transcription license" sections aren't about us. The relevant chapters are derivative works, fair use, and AI authorship.

**Sources:** Passman ch 17 ("Synchronization and Transcription Licenses... transcription licenses... radio, podcasts, audio-only commercials").

## AI authorship and chord chart ownership

### Q: Are the chord charts StemScriber generates copyrightable? Who owns them?

**Probably no copyright owner exists for AI-generated chord charts under current US law.** Per the Copyright Office's 2018 + 2022 + 2023 rulings (Thaler's "Creativity Machine," Kashtanova's *Zarya of the Dawn*, plus the Naruto monkey selfie case), AI-generated output without sufficient human creativity is not copyrightable. The Copyright Office requires human authorship as a prerequisite.

**For StemScriber specifically:**
- The chart is generated by ML models from user-uploaded audio. Minimal human creativity in the *output* itself.
- Neither StemScriber, nor the user, nor the original artist owns a copyright in the *chart as a chart*.
- The *underlying composition* (the song the chart describes) remains copyrighted — that does not change.

**What this gives us:**
- Defensive: competitors copying our chart output have no copyright claim against us; we don't own it either.
- Framing: strengthens the "we are a tool, not a publisher" framing in ToS.

**What this does NOT give us:**
- Permission to ingest copyrighted INPUTS without authorization (that's a separate question, governed by the user-warranty ToS shift and the transformative-use argument).
- The right to host a public library of "Black Cow chord chart" pages (the underlying composition rights are still active — this is why the stored chord library was deleted Apr 10 per Alexandra's directive).

**Sources:** Passman ch 19 ("Artificial Intelligence and Monkey Selfies"), citing Naruto v. Slater, No. 16-15469 (9th Cir. 2018), Urantia Foundation v. Maaherra, 114 F.3d 955 (9th Cir. 1997), and Copyright Office refusals of Thaler's "A Recent Entrance to Paradise" and the Kashtanova *Zarya of the Dawn* registration. Borovick line 4893 ("human authorship is a prerequisite to copyright protection"). Decided 2026-04-30.

**Re-evaluate when:** Thaler v. Perlmutter or related cases produce a different ruling at appellate or SCOTUS level. *Bartz v. Anthropic* and *Concord v. Anthropic* outcomes may also shift the landscape. Track via Court Listener.

## Lyrics

### Q: Can StemScriber transcribe lyrics?

**No — disabled in production.** Karaoke / lyrics transcription requires a separate licensing arrangement with publishers (NMPA / LyricFind / Musixmatch model). The Genius Media v. Google case and the broader NMPA enforcement pattern show that public lyrics hosting is a high-litigation surface area.

**Decision:** Karaoke feature is gated off. Lyrics may appear in user-facing chord charts only as Whisper-generated output for the user's session, not stored or distributed.

**Sources:** Alexandra Mayo direct guidance, Apr 10 2026 call. Confirmed via Goldman casebook §230 / DMCA chapters: lyrics-hosting cases (Genius v. Google, NMPA v. various) consistently find against unlicensed lyrics distribution.

**Re-evaluate when:** StemScriber considers a paid karaoke tier and is willing to license via LyricFind/MLC; or if Concord v. Anthropic produces a precedent around AI-extracted lyrics.

## Stored chord library

### Q: Can StemScriber host a public library of chord charts for popular songs?

**No.** Even though AI-generated chord charts are uncopyrightable in themselves, the underlying *compositions* remain copyrighted. A public library of "[Song] chord chart" pages is functionally equivalent to the Ultimate Guitar / Genius posture that has drawn lawsuits — public hosting of derivatives of copyrighted works.

**Decision:** Per-user, ephemeral processing only. No public chord library. The pre-existing 15,417-chart library was deleted on Apr 16, 2026 per Alexandra's direct guidance. Only 20 Kozelski-original charts remain (Jeff owns those compositions).

**Sources:** Alexandra Mayo direct guidance, Apr 10 2026 call. Goldman casebook covers analogous platform cases (Tiffany v. eBay, Lerner & Rowe v. Brown). NMPA enforcement pattern against public chord/tab/lyric sites is well-documented.

**Re-evaluate when:** Never, unless StemScriber pivots to a fully-licensed library model (which would require negotiating with publishers).

## YouTube URL acceptance

### Q: Should StemScriber accept YouTube URLs as an input source (in addition to file uploads)?

**Yes, with architectural guardrails.** Decision made 2026-04-30 after research across Passman, Goldman, Borovick, and review of industry practice (Klangio, Moises, Chordify, AudioShake all accept YouTube URLs).

**Risk decomposition (across four legal layers):**

1. **Copyright (label/publisher exposure)** — User audio is copyrighted regardless of source. Same risk profile as current direct-upload flow. ToS user-warranty + no public hosting + transformative output cover this layer. *No incremental risk vs. status quo.*

2. **YouTube ToS violation** — yt-dlp violates YT's terms of service. This is a contract dispute with Google, **not a copyright case from labels**. Worst case: Google sends a cease-and-desist or IP-blocks the worker. Recoverable. Klangio/Moises/Chordify all live with this.

3. **DMCA §512 safe harbor** — Goldman: §512 is for *hosted* user content. yt-dlp = user-directed processing, not hosting. With architecture as pass-through (no caching, immediate processing, user pastes URL), safe harbor is preserved.

4. **Section 230 (CDA)** — Goldman: §230 likely applies because the user provides the URL. Search-engine analogy. Defensible but not airtight.

**Architectural guardrails (must ship together):**
1. yt-dlp runs on a separate worker / sandboxed environment so a YouTube IP block doesn't tag the main VPS
2. No caching of extracted audio past the user's session — strict pass-through to the transcription pipeline
3. Per-URL attestation flow — user clicks "I have rights to use this content" before processing (strengthens DMCA good-faith and §230 user-directed posture)
4. Manual-upload fallback page documented for when yt-dlp breaks (matches Klangio's escape hatch)
5. ToS clause explicitly covering URL-pasted content (user warrants rights, including for URLs)
6. **UX framing: file upload is primary, YouTube URL is secondary/fallback.** Decided 2026-04-30 by Jeff. Default upload-UI promotes file upload ("Got the file? Drop it here"). YouTube URL field is presented as a fallback ("No file? Paste a YouTube link below"). This is *not* cosmetic — making YouTube the deliberate-secondary-choice strengthens the §230 user-directed posture (user actively opts into the secondary path) and the user-warranty attestation (user consciously chose it because they didn't have the file). Mirror-image of Klangio's posture (they fall back to manual download when yt-dlp breaks; we invert: file is primary, YouTube is the courtesy fallback).

**Why this is decidable without billing Alexandra:**
- Industry standard (4+ direct competitors all do it)
- Existing legal posture (ToS shift, DMCA registration, no public storage) provides defense in depth
- Books cited (Passman ch 17/19, Goldman §230 + §512, Borovick ch 6) all converge on the same risk profile
- The question is operational architecture, not novel legal interpretation

**Sources:** Passman ch 17 (no transcription license needed; framework is reproduction + derivative + fair use), Passman ch 19 (AI authorship + monkey selfie + training-data lawsuits), Goldman §230 chapter (Zeran v. AOL, Twitter v. Taamneh, Anderson v. TikTok 2024), Goldman §512 chapter (DMCA safe harbor mechanics), Borovick ch 6 (AI training data + transformative use), industry observation (Klangio docs at klang.io/help/audio-youtube-into-sheet/, Moises product, Chordify product).

**Re-evaluate when:** Bartz v. Anthropic or Concord v. Anthropic produces a definitive ruling on AI training-data ingestion that changes the transformative-use calculus; a major label sues a peer competitor (Klangio, Moises, etc.) over yt-dlp specifically; YouTube changes its enforcement posture against transcription tools.

**Optional belt-and-suspenders:** Send Alexandra a *FYI-only* email (not consultation) describing the implementation and architecture. Frame as notice, not request for opinion. Cost: 0.1 hours of her time at most ($40); creates a paper trail showing good-faith disclosure to counsel.

## Klangio precedent (informational reference)

### Q: How does Klangio "get away with" YouTube URL acceptance?

A combination of jurisdictional and architectural factors:

1. **EU jurisdiction.** Klangio is based in Karlsruhe, Germany. EU's DSM Directive Article 4 (2019) provides explicit text-and-data-mining exceptions that let processors crunch copyrighted content for any purpose unless the rightsholder opts out machine-readably. US has nothing comparable.
2. **Output is transformative** — sheet music / MIDI / MusicXML, not the recording. Strong fair-use / fair-dealing argument under both jurisdictions.
3. **No public library of transcribed copyrighted songs.** Same posture StemScriber adopts. Avoids the Ultimate Guitar / Genius lawsuit pattern.
4. **Risk shifted to user via ToS** — "personal use only, you're responsible for rights." Standard pattern.
5. **Below the radar** — small B2B/prosumer scale doesn't justify label legal budgets.
6. **YouTube ToS violation is technical, not copyright.** Different enforcement track.

**Note for StemScriber:** Klangio's posture is workable for them in part because of EU law. StemScriber, in the US, has slightly worse legal cover on the training-data side but identical cover on the transformative-output side. The architectural pattern translates; the jurisdictional advantage doesn't.

**Sources:** Klangio docs (klang.io/help/audio-youtube-into-sheet/, klang.io/transcription-studio/); Borovick ch 6 on EU/UK TDM exceptions; Passman ch 19 on transformative-use precedent (Authors Guild v. Google, Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith).
